## 1 Introduction

Does improving teacher attendance change recorded departure from the original school more for children who could already take a written test? I examine this question in the camera-monitoring and attendance-pay experiment studied by Duflo, Hanna, and Ryan (2012). I reproduce the original attendance and average departure results, then interact randomized treatment with baseline written-test status. The question connects the literature on provider incentives with evidence that children's initial preparation can shape an educational intervention's effects.

The main finding is a difference of 21.1 percentage points between the two groups' treatment effects on recorded departure. The estimated effect is 5.0 points among children who could not take the written baseline test and 26.1 points among children who took it. The direct comparison has a school-clustered p-value of .004 and a 95 percent confidence interval from 6.9 to 35.3 points; a wild-cluster bootstrap gives p = .007. The outcome is the original paper's attendance-based classification of departure from a nonformal education center. Its educational meaning requires separate consideration.

Teacher absence is a substantive constraint on access to instruction in low-resource settings. Unannounced visits to Indian government primary schools found approximately one-quarter of teachers absent and only about half teaching when observed (Kremer et al. 2005). Those descriptive findings establish the scale of the problem rather than the effect of any particular remedy. Guerrero et al. (2013) synthesize nine experimental or quasi-experimental studies of attendance interventions. Their review finds positive pooled attendance effects for monitoring with incentives and for community or parent interventions, while the available pooled achievement effects are statistically inconclusive. Its limited, heterogeneous evidence base and search ending in 2010 caution against assuming that improved presence necessarily delivers every downstream educational benefit.

The margin rewarded by an incentive also matters. Glewwe, Ilias, and Kremer (2010) find that Kenyan test-score incentives increased performance on rewarded examinations and test preparation without increasing teacher attendance or improving unrelated tests. In Indian government schools, Muralidharan and Sundararaman (2011) find learning gains from performance pay without a corresponding attendance change; their evidence points to greater effort conditional on presence. These studies distinguish attendance-linked pay from rewards tied to student performance. The Rajasthan experiment targets presence directly, combining camera records with an attendance payment schedule. Its randomized assignment identifies the package's effect, while the comparison literature explains why results from other incentive designs need not predict its participation outcomes.

The closest additional participation study is Cilliers et al. (2018). In Ugandan primary schools, local monitoring alone did not improve teacher attendance, whereas monitoring combined with a bonus increased presence and improved the quality of reported information. The authors also find enrollment and attainment gains associated with reduced dropout. Changing pupil composition, however, makes learning effects difficult to identify: their selection bounds include zero. This evidence motivates examining children's participation alongside provider presence and test scores. It does not validate the Rajasthan departure indicator. Remaining enrolled in a Ugandan primary school and ceasing to appear in the original nonformal center are different outcomes, with different possibilities for educational progression.

Initial preparation provides a plausible dimension along which responses may differ. Banerjee et al. (2007) find that remedial instruction in urban India produced its largest gains among children at the bottom of the test-score distribution. Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer (2011) find that Kenyan tracking benefited pupils across the initial-achievement distribution. They interpret the results as consistent with peer effects and teachers adapting instruction to pupil level. Together, these studies motivate asking how an intervention interacts with children's starting position, without supplying a universal prediction about the direction of heterogeneity. In the present data, the baseline protocol gave a written test to children who could write and an oral test to those who could not. Written-test status therefore marks initial preparation, although it is not a comprehensive literacy measure. Children who already take a written test may be closer to progressing beyond basic nonformal instruction; others may need more instruction in the original center. These are possible interpretations to examine, rather than identified mechanisms.

Participation measures themselves need scrutiny. Linden and Shastry (2012) compare teacher and external-monitor attendance records under a conditional grain program and document child-specific discretion in recorded attendance. Their evidence establishes that recorded presence and independently observed presence can diverge in another institutional setting. It does not establish manipulation in the camera experiment or in this reanalysis. Here the immediate concern is the roster rule: departure means zero attendance in the latest up to five observed open-school checks. Because treatment changes school opening, it changes opportunities for observing children. Nonformal centers can also prepare pupils to move into government schools. Recorded departure can therefore accompany progression, prolonged absence, or disengagement, and incomplete destination records prevent a complete distinction among these possibilities.

The contribution is a specific comparison using existing observations. Duflo, Hanna, and Ryan (2012) already report aggregate departure in Table 10 and use baseline writing status in attendance and learning analyses. The audited article, supplemental appendix, erratum, and replication programs do not report a baseline-writing interaction for Table 10 departure. This bounded claim concerns those materials; the literature scan does not establish priority across all education research. The extension asks whether the original aggregate classification conceals different responses across preparation groups.

Two qualifications govern the analysis. The research direction was selected after examining several extensions, so all inference remains exploratory, including adjustment within the three departure-related outcomes. The implemented departure measure also remains subject to treatment-dependent observation, incomplete histories, and coding ambiguity. I preserve it for replication and report sensitivities to documented weaknesses. Section 2 presents the original experiment and replication. Section 3 develops the interaction and its results. Section 4 discusses interpretation, and the appendix reports sensitivity and reproducibility details.
