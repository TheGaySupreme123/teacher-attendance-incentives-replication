## Verified peer-reviewed articles

### 1. Banerjee, Cole, Duflo, and Linden (2007) — main include

**Citation and metadata.** Abhijit V. Banerjee, Shawn Cole, Esther Duflo, and Leigh Linden. “Remedying Education: Evidence from Two Randomized Experiments in India.” *The Quarterly Journal of Economics* 122(3), August 2007, 1235–1264. DOI: [10.1162/qjec.122.3.1235](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.122.3.1235). [Oxford Academic publisher record](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/122/3/1235/1879525?login=false). [Public final published-layout PDF hosted by J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/6%20Computer-Assisted%20Learning%20Project%20with%20Pratham%20in%20India%2007.pdf). **Reading depth:** final published-layout PDF, including the abstract and introduction (pp. 1235–1238), program description (pp. 1238–1243), evaluation design and sample (pp. 1240–1247), short-run results (pp. 1248–1253), longer-run results (pp. 1253–1255), heterogeneous effects by initial pretest score (Table V), and conclusion (pp. 1261–1263); publisher metadata cross-checked.

The paper reports two randomized education interventions in urban India. A remedial program hired young women to teach children lagging in basic literacy and numeracy; a computer-assisted program focused on mathematics. The publisher abstract says the remedial intervention raised average test scores by 0.28 standard deviations, with the largest gains among children at the bottom of the test-score distribution. The computer program raised math scores by 0.47 standard deviations; one year later, targeted-child gains persisted but had faded to about 0.10 standard deviations. The paper is a strong conceptual precedent for treating initial literacy/numeracy preparation as substantively meaningful heterogeneity rather than as a nuisance control. It also helps separate a child-level initial-preparation margin from the treatment itself: low preparation can predict larger gains without implying that preparation causes the response.

**Relevance and use.** Main include for the motivation that baseline writing status can proxy meaningful initial learning position. The final article supports the more precise claim that the remedial program's gains were concentrated among initially weaker children, while the computer-assisted program was effective across the initial distribution; it also reports no discernible attendance or dropout effect for either program. It studies learning interventions rather than NFE departure, so it cannot support a claim about schooling exit or attendance-derived records.

### 2. Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer (2011) — main include

**Citation and metadata.** Esther Duflo, Pascaline Dupas, and Michael Kremer. “Peer Effects, Teacher Incentives, and the Impact of Tracking: Evidence from a Randomized Evaluation in Kenya.” *American Economic Review* 101(5), August 2011, 1739–1774. DOI: [10.1257/aer.101.5.1739](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.5.1739). [AEA publisher record](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?from=j&id=10.1257%2Faer.101.5.1739&journal=1&page=136&per-page=21&q=Stiglitz+J&within%5Bauthor%5D=on). [Public final published-layout PDF hosted by J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/39%20Peer%20Effects%2C%20Teacher%20Incentives%2C%20and%20the%20Impact%20of%20Tracking%20Project.pdf). **Reading depth:** final published-layout PDF, including the abstract and introduction (pp. 1739–1743), model (pp. 1743–1748), experimental design and sample (pp. 1749–1751), test-score results and heterogeneous effects by initial achievement (pp. 1756–1764), teacher-response section and Table 6 (pp. 1765–1768), and conclusion (pp. 1769–1774); publisher metadata cross-checked.

This randomized Kenyan study examines tracking, peer composition, and teacher incentives. Tracking raised endline scores by about 0.14 standard deviations overall and benefited students throughout the initial achievement distribution; the paper interprets the pattern as consistent with direct peer effects plus teacher adaptation. The teacher-response section reports higher presence and classroom teaching in tracking schools, with stronger responses in upper than lower tracks and a pattern consistent with convex teacher payoffs. This is closely relevant to the current paper's interpretation of baseline writing as a preparation margin. It supplies a credible reason to ask whether a program's effect differs by initial child preparation, without claiming that the observed writing indicator is a pure skill measure or a mechanism. It also cautions against reading a subgroup interaction as a simple “strong children benefit” story: institutional response and instructional targeting can reverse the expected distributional pattern.

**Relevance and use.** Main include in the introduction or discussion of differential response by initial preparation. Use it for the logic of heterogeneous treatment effects, not for evidence about NFE departure, recorded exit, or Rajasthan measurement.

### 3. Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer (2015) — reserve, with a possible mechanisms paragraph

**Citation and metadata.** Esther Duflo, Pascaline Dupas, and Michael Kremer. “School Governance, Teacher Incentives, and Pupil–Teacher Ratios: Experimental Evidence from Kenyan Primary Schools.” *Journal of Public Economics* 123, March 2015, 92–110. DOI: [10.1016/j.jpubeco.2014.11.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2014.11.008). [ScienceDirect publisher record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047272714002412). **Reading depth:** publisher abstract and substantial HTML sections were accessible; the PDF route was not independently verified.

The experiment randomly assigned Kenyan schools to an extra contract teacher and, in a subset, school-based management training. The publisher abstract reports that reducing the grade-1 pupil–teacher ratio from 82 to 44 did not improve scores for pupils who remained with civil-service teachers, while pupils assigned to contract teachers gained 0.24 standard deviations. The article attributes part of the contrast to effort and governance: civil-service teachers reduced effort when contract teachers arrived, and community empowerment reduced capture of contract-teacher positions. This is relevant to a broad discussion of why the same school intervention can produce different results across children and providers, and why observed participation outcomes may reflect institutional responses. It does not study baseline writing, NFE departure, or an attendance-derived exit rule.

**Relevance and use.** Reserve unless the paper adds a short paragraph on governance and implementation. It is less direct than Duflo–Dupas–Kremer (2011) for child preparation and less direct than Linden–Shastry for measurement.

### 4. Burde and Linden (2013) — reserve/context comparison

**Citation and metadata.** Dana Burde and Leigh L. Linden. “Bringing Education to Afghan Girls: A Randomized Controlled Trial of Village-Based Schools.” *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* 5(3), July 2013, 27–40. DOI: [10.1257/app.5.3.27](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.5.3.27). [AEA publisher record](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fapp.5.3.27&page=531). **Reading depth:** publisher abstract; the AEA full-text PDF requires member, institutional, or purchase access through the route checked.

The randomized trial evaluates village-based schools in rural northwestern Afghanistan using 31 villages and 1,490 children. The publisher abstract reports large gains in enrollment and test scores for all children, with larger effects for girls: enrollment rose by 52 percentage points and test scores by 0.65 standard deviations for girls, compared with 35 percentage points and 0.40 standard deviations for boys. The paper is relevant because it places schooling participation and learning in the same evaluation and shows that access interventions can produce sharply different effects across children. It is also a useful reminder that participation outcomes are not automatically welfare losses or gains: the direction and meaning depend on what schooling option the intervention creates. It does not estimate treatment heterogeneity by baseline writing and does not measure exit from an existing NFE through attendance records.

**Relevance and use.** Reserve for a short contextual comparison if the introduction discusses enrollment/participation versus learning. Do not use it as direct support for the NFE-departure estimand or for a baseline-writing mechanism.

### 5. Evans and Popova (2016) — reserve synthesis

**Citation and metadata.** David K. Evans and Anna Popova. “What Really Works to Improve Learning in Developing Countries? An Analysis of Divergent Findings in Systematic Reviews.” *The World Bank Research Observer* 31(2), August 2016, 242–270. DOI: [10.1093/wbro/lkw004](https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkw004). [Oxford Academic publisher record](https://academic.oup.com/wbro/article-abstract/31/2/242/2418055). **Reading depth:** publisher abstract plus accessible full text in the World Bank's open issue PDF: [World Bank PDF](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/998471565630022176/pdf/The-World-Bank-Research-Observer-31-2.pdf).

This peer-reviewed review compares six reviews of interventions intended to improve learning in developing countries. The publisher abstract says that divergent recommendations are driven largely by differences in which studies each review includes; the most inclusive review incorporated fewer than half of 229 studies with learning results. Across reviews, interventions that tailor teaching to student learning levels and repeated teacher training associated with a specific task or tool receive the most consistent support. The paper is useful for framing why the current paper should distinguish a narrow, pre-treatment preparation interaction from broad claims about “what works.” It also supports disciplined language around evidence synthesis: a positive result in one domain, setting, or outcome family does not establish a general mechanism. Because it is a review of reviews rather than a child-level experiment, it cannot validate the current recorded-departure measure or identify the effect of baseline writing.

**Relevance and use.** Reserve for the introduction if a broad evidence-synthesis sentence is needed; otherwise omit to keep the seminar paper focused. Full text is available through the linked World Bank copy, while the publisher page supplies the journal metadata and abstract.

### 6. Ganimian and Murnane (2016) — reserve synthesis

**Citation and metadata.** Alejandro J. Ganimian and Richard J. Murnane. “Improving Education in Developing Countries: Lessons From Rigorous Impact Evaluations.” *Review of Educational Research* 86(3), September 2016, 719–755. DOI: [10.3102/0034654315627499](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315627499). [Sage publisher record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654315627499?rss=1&ssource=mfr). **Reading depth:** publisher abstract only; the Sage record marks the item as a restricted-access research article. The record identifies it as a review of 223 rigorous evaluations in 56 low- and middle-income countries.

The review groups rigorous evaluations by theory of action and derives four lessons. The publisher abstract reports that reducing schooling costs and expanding schooling options increase attendance and attainment but do not consistently increase achievement; information interventions affect parents and children; resources improve achievement only when they change children's daily school experience; and well-designed incentives can raise teacher effort and student achievement from low levels, although low-skilled teachers need specific instructional guidance. These points help position the current paper's outcome split: recorded NFE departure is a participation-related outcome, while the earlier Direction 0.2 extension concerns learning. The review also supports the caution that an initial child characteristic may index the setting in which an intervention is effective rather than a causal mechanism.

**Relevance and use.** Reserve, and use only for broad framing if Evans–Popova is insufficient. Do not cite the abstract as if it supplied a direct estimate for baseline writing or NFE departure. The publisher page and abstract are enough for a cautious review citation; TAU access would be needed for detailed claims.

### 7. Linden and Shastry (2012) — main include in the measurement paragraph; otherwise reserve

**Citation and metadata.** Leigh L. Linden and Gauri Kartini Shastry. “Grain Inflation: Identifying Agent Discretion in Response to a Conditional School Nutrition Program.” *Journal of Development Economics* 99(1), September 2012, 128–138. DOI: [10.1016/j.jdeveco.2011.11.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2011.11.002). [ScienceDirect publisher record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387811001040). **Reading depth:** publisher abstract and article sections, plus an accessible author-hosted copy of the published layout: [PDF](https://www.leighlinden.com/Grain_Inflation_JDE.pdf).

Using overlapping teacher and external-monitor attendance records in Mumbai, the paper studies a conditional grain program that awarded benefits when a child crossed an 80-percent attendance threshold. Teachers manipulated attendance records in ways that changed which children qualified. The abstract reports differential manipulation: more inflation for girls, better students, and lower-caste students, and less for Muslim students. The article's design is valuable for the current paper because it separates a recorded attendance measure from an independently monitored record and shows that agent discretion can be correlated with child characteristics. That is a measurement warning, not evidence that the current NFE-departure treatment effect is fabricated or that the Duflo–Hanna–Ryan program used the same manipulation. The current paper should use this paper to justify calling `drop` an operational recorded departure under a roster rule and to avoid treating every recorded absence as a directly verified schooling exit.

**Relevance and use.** Main include in one concise measurement paragraph, subject to keeping the distinction between conditional-grain record manipulation and the current camera/open-check outcome explicit. If space is tight, reserve it rather than expanding the mechanism claim.

## Recommended use in the current paper

**Main bibliography:** Banerjee et al. (2007) for initial learning position; Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer (2011) for differential response by initial achievement and instructional targeting; Linden and Shastry (2012) for measurement discretion and the difference between recorded and externally observed attendance.

**Reserve/context:** Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer (2015) for governance and effort; Burde and Linden (2013) for participation versus learning in an access intervention; Evans and Popova (2016) and Ganimian and Murnane (2016) for broad evidence synthesis.

**Do not overuse:** None of these papers validates calling the current operational `drop` indicator “educational dropout,” identifies a mechanism through baseline writing, or establishes that the treatment changes schooling preferences. The main result remains a treatment × baseline-writing difference in recorded NFE departure under the original last-five-open-check rule.

## Suggested compact literature paragraph

> Prior randomized education research shows why initial preparation can matter for treatment response without being a causal mechanism. Remedial instruction in India generated its largest gains among children at the bottom of the initial test-score distribution (Banerjee et al. 2007), and tracking in Kenya benefited lower-achieving pupils when teachers could tailor instruction to their level (Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer 2011). Participation and learning can nevertheless move differently: village-based schools in Afghanistan produced large enrollment and test-score gains with stronger effects for girls (Burde and Linden 2013). The current study asks a different, narrower question about whether the attendance program's recorded NFE-departure response differs by baseline writing status. Because departure is constructed from attendance records, measurement deserves separate attention. Linden and Shastry (2012) show that teachers can exercise child-specific discretion in attendance records when benefits depend on crossing a threshold; their result motivates caution about interpreting recorded absence as verified schooling exit, but does not establish manipulation in the present experiment.
