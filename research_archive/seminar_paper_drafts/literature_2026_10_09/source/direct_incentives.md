# Direct incentives and attendance literature dossier

Prepared 2026-10-09 for the seminar paper expansion.

## Scope and screen

This dossier verifies primary publisher or journal issue pages for journal literature on teacher attendance monitoring, performance pay, unconditional salary, and closely related public-service incentive designs. It does not use TAU Primo; TAU catalog and library screening are handled separately by the parent task.

The seminar outcome is **recorded operational NFE departure**. The literature below does not authorize relabeling that outcome as teacher absence, teacher exit, student dropout, or educational attainment.

For the strict peer-reviewed screen, I included articles in JEEA, AEJ: Applied Economics, JPE, QJE, Journal of Public Economics, Journal of Labor Economics, and Journal of Development Effectiveness. I list JEP pieces separately because the AEA's JEP editorial policy says the journal is primarily solicited and sits between refereed economics research journals and the popular press; it does not state a standard external peer-review process for every article.

Official peer-review policies checked:

- [JEEA author guidelines](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/pages/Author_Guidelines): manuscripts not rejected without review are sent for peer review and use single-blind review.
- [QJE policies](https://academic.oup.com/QJE/pages/Policies): manuscripts not desk rejected are usually sent to two independent peer reviewers.
- [JPE instructions for authors](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/journals/jpe/instruct): JPE uses single-blind peer review and outside referees.
- [AEJ: Applied Economics editorial policy](https://www.aeaweb.org/journals/app/editorial-policy): submissions undergo single-blind peer review, subject to editorial returns without referee review.
- [Journal of Development Effectiveness journal page](https://www.tandfonline.com/rjde): after an editor's suitability assessment, research articles are double-anonymized peer reviewed by independent, anonymous expert referees.
- [JEP editorial policy](https://www.aeaweb.org/journals/jep/editorial-policy): context only under the strict filter described above.
- For Elsevier journals, the [Journal of Public Economics article page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272718300719) labels the item a research article; Elsevier describes its peer-review process [here](https://www.elsevier.com/reviewer/what-is-peer-review).

Reading depth is reported honestly: publisher full text, publisher-formatted or version-of-record PDF hosted by an author/institution, or final publisher abstract plus a readable prepublication manuscript.

## Peer-reviewed articles

### Duflo, Hanna, and Ryan (2012)

**Citation:** Esther Duflo, Rema Hanna, and Stephen P. Ryan. “Incentives Work: Getting Teachers to Come to School.” *American Economic Review* 102(4): 1241-1278. DOI: [10.1257/aer.102.4.1241](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.102.4.1241). Publisher page: [AEA article record](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.102.4.1241). Reading depth: local full text plus publisher metadata.

This is the seminar's anchor study. In rural nonformal education centers in Rajasthan, tamper-resistant daily camera records were tied to a nonlinear attendance payment. The intervention reduced teacher absence and increased student test scores. The original paper also reports child-level departure outcomes in Table 10: children left the NFE at rates of 0.44 in treatment and 0.36 in control; government-school enrollment was 0.26 versus 0.16; and the defined school-dropout rate was 0.18 versus 0.20, an insignificant difference. The current extension reuses this Table 10 departure construction and asks whether the treatment effect differs by baseline writing status (`treat × pre_writ`). It is therefore a subgroup-heterogeneity comparison within the same NFE setting, not a different original setting or a new aggregate departure endpoint. Cite it as evidence that a tightly defined attendance incentive changed observed presence and learning in this setting, while keeping the current interaction exploratory and separate from the paper's aggregate Table 10 results.

**Use:** Closest monitoring-and-attendance benchmark.

**Limits:** Different Rajasthan NFE context and intervention; no validation of the current departure coding; no teacher-exit or student-school-dropout outcome.

### Kremer et al. (2005)

**Citation:** Michael Kremer, Nazmul Chaudhury, F. Halsey Rogers, Karthik Muralidharan, and Jeffrey Hammer. “Teacher Absence in India: A Snapshot.” *Journal of the European Economic Association* 3(2-3): 658-667. DOI: [10.1162/jeea.2005.3.2-3.658](https://doi.org/10.1162/jeea.2005.3.2-3.658). [Publisher page](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article-abstract/3/2-3/658/2281517). Reading depth: publisher metadata and abstract; the publisher states the content is PDF-only.

This article supplies a national Indian benchmark for provider absence rather than a randomized incentive estimate. During unannounced visits to a nationally representative sample of government primary schools, about one quarter of teachers were absent and only about half were teaching; absence ranged from 15 percent in Maharashtra to 42 percent in Jharkhand. Higher pay was not associated with lower absence. Recent inspection, better infrastructure, and proximity to a paved road were associated with lower absence, while local ties and PTAs showed little association. The evidence is observational and based on spot checks, so the correlates should not be read as causal treatment effects. It is useful for motivating why monitoring and working conditions matter, but it does not measure NFE departure, teacher turnover, student dropout, or learning responses to an intervention.

**Use:** Descriptive baseline for attendance measurement and monitoring correlates.

**Limits:** Observational; India government primary schools; spot-check absence is not teacher exit or student dropout.

### Banerjee, Duflo, and Glennerster (2008)

**Citation:** Abhijit V. Banerjee, Esther Duflo, and Rachel Glennerster. “Putting a Band-Aid on a Corpse: Incentives for Nurses in the Indian Public Health Care System.” *Journal of the European Economic Association* 6(2-3): 487-500. DOI: [10.1162/JEEA.2008.6.2-3.487](https://doi.org/10.1162/JEEA.2008.6.2-3.487). [Publisher article page](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article/6/2-3/487/2295851). [Readable published-layout PDF](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/documents/band-aid_0.pdf). Reading depth: publisher issue metadata plus full text.

This randomized field experiment is a health-service analogue for monitoring and sanction design. In rural Rajasthan, an NGO used time-and-date-stamped monitoring of auxiliary nurse-midwives and the district administration announced pay deductions and suspension for severe absence. Presence initially rose substantially: for single-nurse centers, monitored-day presence was 59 percent versus 30 percent in controls in the early period. The effect later disappeared as exempt days, machine problems, and administrative non-enforcement increased. The article therefore shows both that providers can respond to a credible attendance incentive and that local implementation can neutralize a rule that remains formally in place. It is not a teacher study and does not measure student outcomes, NFE departure, or educational dropout.

**Use:** Implementation, collusion, and information-quality caveat.

**Limits:** Health workers, not teachers; no education outcome; attendance effect weakened over time.

### Glewwe, Ilias, and Kremer (2010)

**Citation:** Paul Glewwe, Nauman Ilias, and Michael Kremer. “Teacher Incentives.” *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* 2(3): 205-227. DOI: [10.1257/app.2.3.205](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.2.3.205). [AEA article record](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.2.3.205). [Public author-hosted final published-layout PDF](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/kremer/files/teacher_incentives_aej_july_2010_sunday.pdf). Reading depth: final publisher abstract plus author-hosted final published-layout PDF.

This randomized Kenyan school incentive program rewarded teachers using student test performance and penalized students who did not take exams. Test scores rose on incentivized exams, but most gains were concentrated on the reward formula; unrelated tests did not improve. Dropout was unchanged, while exam participation increased. Teacher attendance and homework assignment were unaffected, but test-preparation sessions increased, and gains did not persist after the program ended. It is a central warning that performance pay can shift effort toward measured outcomes without changing attendance or broad learning. It is not an attendance-monitoring experiment, and its dropout variable concerns enrolled pupils, not provider departure. The final AEA abstract and a publicly accessible author-hosted final published-layout PDF were verified; the evidence still applies to the published article's Kenyan design, not to the current NFE departure endpoint.

**Use:** Comparator showing that test-score incentives and attendance incentives operate through different margins.

**Limits:** Kenya primary schools; student-test incentive; no NFE departure.

### Muralidharan and Sundararaman (2011)

**Citation:** Karthik Muralidharan and Venkatesh Sundararaman. “Teacher Performance Pay: Experimental Evidence from India.” *Journal of Political Economy* 119(1): 39-77. DOI: [10.1086/659655](https://doi.org/10.1086/659655). [Publisher page](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/659655). [Publisher-formatted PDF hosted by the author](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~kamurali/papers/Published%20Articles/Publisher%20Formatted%20Versions/Teacher%20Performance%20Pay%20(JPE%202011).pdf). Reading depth: publisher metadata plus publisher-formatted version-of-record PDF.

This large randomized evaluation covered 300 rural government primary schools in Andhra Pradesh, with individual and group performance-pay arms and a control group. After two years, incentive schools improved mathematics and language scores by about 0.27 and 0.17 standard deviations, with individual incentives outperforming group incentives in the second year. The paper reports no difference in student or teacher attendance, no treatment difference in student attrition, and no adverse gaming on its checks. Direct observations and interviews suggest the main mechanism was greater or more effective effort conditional on being present, including extra classes, homework, and test preparation, rather than more attendance. This is a strong India comparator for performance pay, but its teacher-attendance null cannot be transferred to the current operational departure measure.

**Use:** India benchmark for performance pay, conditional effort, attendance nulls, and attrition checks.

**Limits:** Government rural primary schools; incentive tied to learning, not presence; student attrition is test-taking attrition, not teacher/NFE departure.

### de Ree, Muralidharan, Pradhan, and Rogers (2018)

**Citation:** Joppe de Ree, Karthik Muralidharan, Menno Pradhan, and Halsey Rogers. “Double for Nothing? Experimental Evidence on an Unconditional Teacher Salary Increase in Indonesia.” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 133(2): 993-1039. DOI: [10.1093/qje/qjx040](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjx040). [Publisher article page](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/133/2/993/4622956). [Version-of-record PDF hosted by VU](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/127419178/Double_for_nothing_Experimental_evidence_on_an_unconditional_teacher_salary_increase_in_Indonesia.pdf). Reading depth: publisher issue metadata plus version-of-record PDF from an institutional repository.

This large randomized Indonesian experiment accelerated access to a permanent doubling of eligible civil-service teachers' base salaries. The pay increase improved teacher income satisfaction, reduced financial stress, and reduced outside jobs, but after two and three years did not improve teacher effort or student learning. The published article reports no consistent impact on self-reported attendance and precise null learning effects. The study separates unconditional pay from monitoring or performance-linked pay: improving welfare and available time did not automatically change incumbent effort. It is a comparison of compensation structure, not a teacher-exit or NFE-departure study. It also cautions against using a null learning effect as evidence that all incentives fail; this treatment changed salary without changing the information or conditionality attached to performance.

**Use:** Mechanism contrast: unconditional salary is not equivalent to attendance monitoring or performance pay.

**Limits:** Indonesia civil-service reform; self-reported attendance; no departure outcome.

### Mbiti et al. (2019)

**Citation:** Isaac Mbiti, Karthik Muralidharan, Mauricio Romero, Youdi Schipper, Constantine Manda, and Rakesh Rajani. “Inputs, Incentives, and Complementarities in Education: Experimental Evidence from Tanzania.” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 134(3): 1627-1673. DOI: [10.1093/qje/qjz010](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz010). [Publisher full text](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/134/3/1627/5479257). Reading depth: publisher full-text HTML.

This factorial randomized experiment assigned 350 Tanzanian public schools to grants, teacher performance pay, both, or control. Grant-only schools did not improve low-stakes test scores; teacher incentives produced mixed effects, with larger effects on high-stakes tests used for bonuses; the combination produced positive low- and high-stakes effects. The interaction between inputs and incentives was positive and statistically significant, showing experimentally identified complementarity rather than merely correlational resource heterogeneity. The article discusses threshold incentives, possible teaching-to-the-test, implementation capacity, and differences between incentivized and nonincentivized tests. It does not study teacher attendance or departure. It is useful for a synthesis that treats monitoring/pay as one component of a broader production system, while avoiding the claim that a positive learning effect from combined inputs is evidence for the current departure mechanism.

**Use:** Systems synthesis: incentive effects may depend on complementary resources and measurement design.

**Limits:** Tanzania schools; incentives based on student tests; no NFE departure or teacher-exit outcome.

### Cilliers et al. (2018)

**Citation:** Jacobus Cilliers, Ibrahim Kasirye, Clare Leaver, Pieter Serneels, and Andrew Zeitlin. “Pay for locally monitored performance? A welfare analysis for teacher attendance in Ugandan primary schools.” *Journal of Public Economics* 167: 69-90. DOI: [10.1016/j.jpubeco.2018.04.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2018.04.010). [ScienceDirect publisher page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272718300719). [Public coauthor-hosted final published-layout PDF](https://andrewzeitlin.io/assets/pdf/Pay%20for%20Locally%20Monitored%20Performance%20-%20JPubEc2018.pdf). [Earlier IZA discussion-paper PDF](https://docs.iza.org/dp10118.pdf). **Reading depth:** TAU-confirmed final publisher full text, including the HTML body and appendices, cross-checked against the public coauthor-hosted final published-layout PDF; the IZA PDF was used only for version comparison.

This is the strongest newly verified direct comparator for the seminar's monitoring question. In a randomized Ugandan primary-school experiment, 20 schools received mobile attendance information and feedback, 25 received the same monitoring plus a monthly bonus, and 40 were controls. Local monitoring alone did not improve attendance. Adding financial stakes increased independently spot-checked teacher presence by about nine percentage points (reported in the abstract as eight) and improved the quality of information. The study also observed enrollment gains, concentrated where dropout was common, but its Lee bounds span zero for learning because treatment changed pupil composition. The authors' welfare analysis finds the design fiscally and socially sustainable under their assumptions. The Ugandan outcome is school enrollment/attainment, not an operational NFE-departure code. Exact values and caveats here are taken from the final published-layout article; the earlier IZA version is retained only as a historical working-paper record.

**Use:** Direct local-monitoring-plus-bonus comparator; information quality and downstream-enrollment caveat.

**Limits:** Ugandan government primary schools; spot-check attendance and enrollment outcomes; enrollment/dropout composition is not the seminar's departure measure.

### Guerrero, León, Zapata, and Cueto (2013)

**Citation:** Gabriela Guerrero, Juan León, Mayli Zapata, and Santiago Cueto. “Getting Teachers Back to the Classroom: A Systematic Review on What Works to Improve Teacher Attendance in Developing Countries.” *Journal of Development Effectiveness* 5(4): 466-488. DOI: [10.1080/19439342.2013.864695](https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2013.864695). [Publisher full text](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19439342.2013.864695). [Publisher PDF](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/19439342.2013.864695). Reading depth: publisher full-text HTML/PDF and journal peer-review policy.

**Summary (conservative):** This systematic review searched 18,524 records and, after screening, included nine experimental or quasi-experimental studies of teacher-attendance interventions in developing-country primary or secondary schools. Six studies supplied enough information for statistical synthesis. Random-effects pooled effects on teacher attendance were positive for direct programs coupling monitoring with incentives (0.30 SD) and for indirect programs involving communities or parents (0.16 SD); pooled effects on student achievement were not statistically significant. The review supports a cautious synthesis that monitoring and incentives can affect observed attendance in some settings. It is a synthesis of prior studies, not a new causal estimate, and it does not measure teacher exit, child departure, or NFE outcomes. The publisher states that research articles undergo double-anonymized review by independent anonymous expert referees.

**Limits:** The search covered studies published through July 2010, leaving only nine included studies; three could not enter the meta-analysis because required statistics were unavailable. Countries, interventions, attendance measures, and study designs were heterogeneous. Effect sizes were selected and aggregated at the study level, and publication bias was assessed with a visual funnel plot rather than ruled out. Several included records were earlier working-paper versions of studies later published elsewhere. Use this article to organize mechanisms and study selection, not to transfer its pooled estimate to the current `treat × pre_writ` departure interaction.

**Use:** Peer-reviewed synthesis for the attendance-monitoring literature; not a direct outcome or mechanism match.

### Fryer (2013)

**Citation:** Roland G. Fryer. “Teacher Incentives and Student Achievement: Evidence from New York City Public Schools.” *Journal of Labor Economics* 31(2): 373-407. DOI: [10.1086/667757](https://doi.org/10.1086/667757). [Publisher page](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/667757). [Readable author manuscript](https://fryer.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5986/files/fryer/files/teacher_incentives_and_student_achievement_evidence_from_new_york_city_public_schools.pdf). Reading depth: final publisher abstract plus author manuscript (2011 version).

This randomized New York City school-level incentive trial covered more than 200 public schools and used school performance measures tied to a flexible compensation design. The publisher's final abstract reports no evidence that incentives increased student performance, attendance, or graduation, and no evidence of changes in student or teacher behavior; if anything, achievement may have fallen, especially in larger schools. The author manuscript explains that schools mostly chose group-based rewards, the performance formula was complex, and teachers may have had little agency or ability to map effort into a predictable payment. This is a useful external-context contrast to low-resource settings where simple attendance-linked incentives can work. It is not a direct test of NFE departure, and the U.S. program's institutional complexity limits direct transport to Rajasthan or Uganda.

**Use:** Reserve as a different-context peer-reviewed contrast.

**Limits:** New York City public schools and school-level bonuses; detailed reading used the author manuscript; no teacher-exit or NFE-departure outcome.

## Screened relevant articles not counted under the strict filter

### Chaudhury et al. (2006)

**Citation:** Nazmul Chaudhury, Jeffrey Hammer, Michael Kremer, Karthik Muralidharan, and F. Halsey Rogers. “Missing in Action: Teacher and Health Worker Absence in Developing Countries.” *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 20(1): 91-116. DOI: [10.1257/089533006776526058](https://doi.org/10.1257/089533006776526058). [Publisher page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/089533006776526058). [Readable full text mirror](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08c2e40f0b64974001052/Chaudhury_etal_Missinginaction_1_.pdf).

The article reports unannounced visits across Bangladesh, Ecuador, India, Indonesia, Peru, and Uganda. Average teacher absence was about 19 percent and health-worker absence about 35 percent; in India, one quarter of government primary teachers were absent and only about 45 percent were engaged in teaching activity when observed. Absence was widespread rather than confined to a few ghost workers, and infrastructure and inspection conditions were associated with absence. This is useful measurement and institutional context, but it is not a causal incentive study. Under a strict peer-reviewed-only rule it should be treated cautiously: the AEA's JEP policy says articles are primarily solicited and places JEP between refereed research journals and the popular press, without stating standard external peer review for every paper. Do not use it as proof of a causal effect or of NFE departure.

### Banerjee and Duflo (2006)

**Citation:** Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo. “Addressing Absence.” *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 20(1): 117-132. DOI: [10.1257/089533006776526139](https://doi.org/10.1257/089533006776526139). [Publisher page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/089533006776526139). [Readable full text mirror](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/addressing_absence.pdf).

This synthesis reviews randomized evidence on external monitoring, attendance-linked incentives, community oversight, and demand-side pressure in education and health. It emphasizes that providers can respond to moderate incentives, but implementation by supervisors and beneficiaries is often the binding constraint. It summarizes the Rajasthan camera-and-pay attendance experiment, the Kenyan test-score incentive study, and local-monitoring efforts, making it a useful bridge between the attendance mechanism and later performance-pay literature. It is a review rather than a new estimate, and the AEA's JEP editorial policy does not state standard external peer review for each article. Under the strict filter it should stay in a contextual screened list or be omitted from the formal peer-reviewed bibliography. It does not measure current operational departure.

## Synthesis for the main draft

A paragraph supported by the strict peer-reviewed set:

> The peer-reviewed evidence is heterogeneous because the interventions target different margins. Attendance-linked monitoring and pay can increase observed teacher presence in some low-resource settings (Duflo, Hanna, and Ryan 2012; Cilliers et al. 2018), whereas student-test incentives may increase scores or test preparation without changing attendance (Glewwe, Ilias, and Kremer 2010; Muralidharan and Sundararaman 2011). Unconditional salary increases can improve teacher welfare without improving effort or learning (de Ree et al. 2018), and a factorial design shows that incentive effects can depend on complementary school inputs (Mbiti et al. 2019). Evidence from a large U.S. school-level program is a useful external contrast: complex group incentives produced no positive achievement, attendance, graduation, or behavior effects (Fryer 2013). The Ugandan local-monitoring experiment is the closest additional comparator because its attendance bonus raised teacher presence and improved information, while downstream enrollment changes altered pupil composition and made learning effects inconclusive (Cilliers et al. 2018).

Drafting cautions:

- Use **recorded operational NFE departure** for the current outcome.
- Do not write “dropout,” “teacher attrition,” “teacher exit,” “attendance,” or “leaving education” unless the specific data rule supports that term.
- Do not infer that the current departure effect operates through attendance, teacher learning, student learning, or retention.
- The literature separates attendance-linked monitoring, test-score performance pay, unconditional salary, and input-complementary incentives. A result in one category does not identify the mechanism in another.
- The Cilliers et al. enrollment/dropout result is a different downstream outcome and cannot validate the current departure coding.
- Keep any novelty claim narrow: the specific outcome construction and analysis in the released replication data, subject to the paper's exploratory status. The search does not establish originality across the full literature.

(2018); Guerrero et al. (2013); de Ree et al. (2018); Mbiti et al. (2019). Optional context: Banerjee, Duflo, and Glennerster (2008) for implementation and information quality; Fryer (2013) for a different institutional setting and null/negative contrast.
